📌 Key Concepts

  • Law of Demand – inverse P-Q relationship
  • Law of Supply – direct P-Q relationship
  • Equilibrium – Qd = Qs, no excess
  • Demand shift factors: Income, tastes, prices of related goods
  • Supply shift factors: Input costs, technology, taxes
  • Consumer surplus = area above P*, below D
  • Producer surplus = area below P*, above S

⚠️ Limitations

  • Assumes ceteris paribus (all else equal)
  • Doesn't account for externalities
  • Static model – no time dimension
  • Assumes rational consumers
  • Ignores information asymmetry
  • Linear representation oversimplifies

🌍 Real World Examples

  • COVID → ↑ Demand for masks → price rose
  • OPEC cuts oil supply → petrol prices up
  • Onion shortage in India → price spike 2023
  • Digital music → ↓ demand for CDs
  • Minimum wage = price floor in labour market

📌 PED Determinants

  • Availability of substitutes (more subs = elastic)
  • Necessity vs luxury
  • Proportion of income spent
  • Time horizon (long run = more elastic)
  • Habit formation (addictive = inelastic)
  • Breadth of market definition

⚠️ Limitations

  • Assumes linear demand (rarely true)
  • Changes along the curve
  • Ignores cross-price & income effects
  • Hard to measure accurately
  • Consumer behaviour is not always rational
  • Dynamic markets change elasticity over time

🌍 Real World Examples

  • Petrol – inelastic (no close substitute)
  • Air travel – elastic (can use train/bus)
  • Insulin – perfectly inelastic
  • Designer handbags – elastic
  • Salt – inelastic (cheap, necessary)

📌 Key Relationships

  • MP > AP → AP is rising
  • MP = AP → AP is at maximum
  • MP < AP → AP is falling
  • TP max when MP = 0
  • TP falls when MP < 0
  • Stage II is rational production zone

⚠️ Limitations

  • Short-run only (one factor fixed)
  • Assumes homogeneous labour units
  • Technology assumed constant
  • Ignores quality of inputs
  • Smooth curves don't exist in reality
  • Doesn't cover multi-output scenarios

🌍 Real World Examples

  • Farms – more workers initially boost crops
  • Factories – too many workers on one machine
  • Software teams – Brooks' Law (more devs ≠ faster)
  • Restaurant kitchen with fixed stoves
  • Call centre with fixed phone lines

📌 Key Rules

  • MC cuts AC & AVC at their minimum points
  • AFC is always downward sloping (hyperbola)
  • AC = AVC + AFC at every output
  • Short-run: FC doesn't change with Q
  • Long-run: All costs are variable
  • Shutdown: P < AVC in short run

⚠️ Limitations

  • U-shape assumes specific production tech
  • Static – doesn't reflect learning by doing
  • Ignores sunk costs in decision-making
  • Economies of scale not shown in SR
  • Doesn't account for joint production
  • Opportunity costs often excluded

🌍 Real World Examples

  • Airlines: high FC (aircraft), variable fuel costs
  • Pharma: high R&D (FC), low marginal pill cost
  • Software: near-zero MC per additional user
  • Textile mills: scale economies reduce AC
  • Hospitals: FC heavy – high breakeven output

📌 Market Comparison

  • PC: P=MR=MC, zero LR profit, price taker
  • Monopoly: P > MR = MC, barriers to entry
  • Oligopoly: few sellers, interdependence
  • Monopolistic: product differentiation
  • All: MR=MC maximises profit
  • Monopsony: buyer market power

⚠️ Limitations

  • Perfect competition rarely exists
  • Ignores game theory in oligopoly
  • Static model – no innovation incentive
  • MC pricing may not cover FC
  • Doesn't capture non-price competition
  • Behavioural economics challenges rationality

🌍 Real World Examples

  • PC: Agricultural produce (wheat, paddy)
  • Monopoly: Indian Railways (pre-reform)
  • Oligopoly: Telecom (Jio, Airtel, Vi)
  • Monopolistic: FMCG, shampoo brands
  • Natural monopoly: Power distribution utilities

📌 Properties of IC

  • Downward sloping (negative MRS)
  • Convex to origin (diminishing MRS)
  • Cannot intersect (transitivity)
  • Higher IC = higher utility
  • Parallel budget lines = income change
  • Rotating budget line = price change

⚠️ Limitations

  • Assumes only 2 goods (restrictive)
  • Cardinal measurement of utility impossible
  • Assumes consistent preferences
  • Ignores complementary/substitute goods
  • Doesn't handle corner solutions well
  • Behavioural biases not captured

🌍 Real World Examples

  • Food vs clothing choice under budget
  • Work-leisure tradeoff (labour economics)
  • Investment: risk vs return portfolio
  • Two brands of cereals – same utility
  • Present vs future consumption (savings)

📌 Policy Effects

  • ↑G → IS shifts right → ↑Y, ↑r (crowding out)
  • ↑M → LM shifts right → ↑Y, ↓r
  • Liquidity trap: LM flat, monetary policy useless
  • Classical range: LM vertical
  • Crowding out: fiscal expansion raises r, reduces I
  • Multiplier effect seen in IS shift

⚠️ Limitations

  • Assumes closed economy (no trade)
  • Static model – no price level changes
  • Doesn't include supply side
  • Extended by Mundell-Fleming for open economy
  • Not applicable at zero lower bound well
  • Ignores expectations (rational or adaptive)

🌍 Real World Examples

  • 2008 crisis: fiscal stimulus → IS right shift
  • COVID RBI rate cuts → LM right shift
  • US QE 2010-2014: massive LM expansion
  • Japan liquidity trap (1990s)
  • India FRBM Act limits G shift (IS)

📌 Gini Interpretation

  • Gini = 0: Perfect equality (all earn same)
  • Gini = 1: Perfect inequality (one person all)
  • 0–0.29: Low inequality
  • 0.30–0.39: Moderate inequality
  • 0.40+: High inequality
  • Used in HDI and poverty analysis

⚠️ Limitations

  • Same Gini can have different Lorenz curves
  • Doesn't show absolute income levels
  • Ignores non-monetary welfare
  • Doesn't capture inter-generational mobility
  • Data collection issues in developing nations
  • Not sensitive to top 1% concentration

🌍 Real World Examples

  • India Gini ~0.35 (2023 estimates)
  • Scandinavian countries ~0.27 (most equal)
  • South Africa ~0.63 (most unequal)
  • Brazil improved from 0.59 to 0.53 (Bolsa)
  • China Gini rose from 0.30→0.47 with growth
